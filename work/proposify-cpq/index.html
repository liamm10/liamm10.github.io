Our real competitor was the spreadsheet, and the spreadsheet was winning.

The v2 pricing library made reps enter every price by hand, even though their price book already lived in a spreadsheet. So they worked around it. Some pasted screenshots of the price book into proposals. Others linked out to a Google Sheet.

Nobody downstream could tell which price was current. The same product went out at different prices across one sales team, with nothing in Proposify to keep them in line.

"I discovered two reps were quoting different prices for the same product. There was no way to enforce consistency."VP Sales, B2B services provider

Most customers didn't need CPQ. They needed one price list.

The original brief was a full CPQ: configuration, pricing, quoting, approvals and guided selling. Fifteen customer interviews, including four with super users at mid-market agencies, showed two different groups behind that brief.

A minority were large franchises that needed pricing logic set up for their industry. Most were agencies that needed one reliable catalog to quote from. Building franchise logic first would have made most customers wait for features they didn't need.

We cut a full CPQ brief down to the part nobody trusted: the price.

The brief was a full CPQ product: configuration, pricing, quoting, approvals and guided selling. Four sprints of interviews argued against building it that way. Reps weren't losing time to configuration logic; they were losing it to not trusting the price they started from. So Phase 1 narrowed to the Pricing Catalog, with the rest of CPQ to follow on top of it.

Four principles the cut committed us to.

Narrowing to a catalog meant every later trade-off had to protect that one price list.

  1. Start from what they already have. Reps' prices already lived in spreadsheets, so the catalog needed a way to bring them in. (Delivered in Phase 2 with CSV import.)
  2. One price list, every proposal. The catalog is the source, and every pricing table pulls from it.
  3. No silent automation. A person makes every change to a price, and the change is visible.
  4. Fail safely. Protect the number without blocking the rep from sending.

I spent two weeks pushing for two-way sync. Then I recommended we kill it.

I started out arguing for live, two-way sync between the catalog and customers' spreadsheets. It looked like the shortest path to one price list.

A feasibility spike with engineering showed the flaw. When both sides changed the same price, any resolution would silently overwrite one of them. That broke our own no-silent-automation principle, in a product where a wrong number ends up in a signed contract.

"We want the system to update prices, but only if we know when it happens. Surprises would erode our clients' trust."Account Director, marketing agency

So I recommended cutting it. Phase 1 shipped a catalog that people maintain themselves, where every change is a deliberate move and conflicts can't happen. Phase 2 added CSV import, so reps could start from the spreadsheet they already had instead of retyping it.

Three safeguards kept pricing consistent across every proposal.

  • Locked on send · Phase 1. Once a proposal is sent, its prices are fixed. The version a client signs is the version they received.
  • Shared pricing tables · Phase 2. Teams save a pricing table once and reuse it across proposals. Only its owner, typically the sales manager, can edit it, so every proposal built from it carries the same prices.
  • Price provenance · Phase 2, with the notifications team. Each line shows where its price came from and flags any override, so a reviewer can see when a proposal departs from the catalog.

Each time the system showed more, whether a log, an icon or an extra confirmation, reps trusted it more.

Building a priced proposal got faster and far more reliable.

Across the rollout cohort, which started with 100 companies, the time from duplicating a template to sending a proposal dropped by about 25%.

For accounts that had fully moved from v2 to v3 but were struggling with v3's pricing tables, task completion rose from 50% to 87.5%. More of them stayed on v3 instead of reverting to v2.

Every proposal a team sent now pulled its prices from the same catalog.

Both measured in product analytics on the rollout cohort.

What the foundation set up.

Phase 1 shipped the catalog most customers needed. Phase 2 let teams bring in their spreadsheets and control who could edit a price. Interviews kept surfacing inconsistent proposals, so Phase 3 added a basic approval workflow and user roles on top of the catalog.

The hardest call on this project was cutting a feature I'd argued for.

Shipping less gave reps one price list they actually used, and what they asked for next, approvals and roles, showed us where CPQ needed to go.
