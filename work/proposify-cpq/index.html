Proposify Pricing Library, Lead Product Designer.

A pricing tool reps would trust more than their own spreadsheet.

~25%Faster assembly
50→87.5%Task completion

What I owned

  • Customer research: interviews across four mid-market accounts, plus support tickets
  • Feasibility on external pricing integrations, with engineering
  • Catalog architecture and pricing UX, from flows to high-fidelity prototypes
  • Rollout, beta, and post-launch evaluation

Constraints

  • 3 months to Phase 1
  • One PM, four engineers, and myself as the product design lead
  • Customers' real prices lived in systems we didn't own
  • A wrong number becomes a signed contract

Our real competitor was Excel, and Excel was winning.

Mid-market reps didn't trust Proposify with their prices. The v2 Pricing Library couldn't bundle, tier, apply conditional discounts, route approvals or sync with an outside price source, so reps built every quote in a spreadsheet and pasted the result in. Nobody downstream could see what had happened to the number.

"I discovered two reps were quoting different prices for the same product. There was no way to enforce consistency."VP Sales, B2B services provider

Customers wanted automation, but never at the cost of seeing what changed.

"We want the system to update prices, but only if we know when it happens. Surprises would erode our clients' trust."Account Director, marketing agency

I turned that into four principles the team used for every trade-off:

  • Simplicity over complexity. Build on the quoting habits people already had.
  • External source of truth. Treat the customer's own pricing system as authoritative, not something to replace.
  • Clarity and autonomy. No silent automation, ever.
  • Trust through fail-safes. Locking, alerts, and predictable behaviour when things go wrong.

We cut a full CPQ brief down to the part nobody trusted: the price.

The brief was a full CPQ product: configuration, pricing, quoting, approvals and guided selling. Four sprints of interviews argued against building it that way. Reps weren't losing time to configuration logic; they were losing it to not trusting the price they started from. So Phase 1 narrowed to the Pricing Catalog, with the rest of CPQ to follow on top of it.

A catalog worth quoting from, wired into every pricing table.

The catalog replaced the flat fee list with folders, search and inline editing, and billing frequency travels with each item instead of being retyped into every quote.

I spent two weeks pushing for two-way pricing sync. Then we killed it.

Customers were asking for live sync between Proposify and their own sheets, and I argued for it. A feasibility spike with engineering found what I'd missed: if both sides changed the same price between syncs, every way of resolving it silently overwrote one of them. That broke the no-silent-automation principle, and a pricing tool that changes numbers without telling you is worse than none, because people stop checking.

Killing sync made the catalog the source of truth inside Proposify, and that tested our second principle. It turned out to be a hypothesis. We'd assumed customers needed Proposify to defer to a pricing system they already had. In reality, smaller companies were happy to build their catalog from scratch, and others were fine uploading a CSV to start. What most companies actually used to track pricing was an Excel sheet, so the eventual answer was simply letting them link it as the source.

Three fail-safes made the numbers trustworthy.

  • Quote locking. Freezes every price once a proposal is sent, so a catalog change can't rewrite what a client already saw.
  • Provenance. Shows where each line's price came from and whether someone overrode it.
  • Approval thresholds. Catch risky quotes without ever blocking the rep.

Building a priced proposal became faster and significantly more reliable.
